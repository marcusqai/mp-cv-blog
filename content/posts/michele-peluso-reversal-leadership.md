> Real leadership is not making every problem look small. It is helping a team face reality honestly and move forward together.

## 1. Leadership Across Industries

Michèle Peluso's career spans retail, e-commerce, travel, financial services, and corporate leadership. She held leadership roles at Travelocity before moving into larger-scale responsibilities at Citi. That cross-industry experience raises an important management question:

**When a leader moves from a fast-growing digital business into a large and complex organization, how can she create meaningful change without simply copying the methods of her previous company?**

The answer is rarely a direct transfer of tools. Every organization has its own history, incentives, decision-making speed, operating habits, and informal power structures. Effective leaders first understand the system, then decide where change is necessary.

## 2. Reversal Does Not Begin with Blame

When a team is underperforming, managers often reach for three familiar responses: more meetings, more detailed reports, and repeated questions about why work is not finished. These actions can be useful in moderation. But when they become the only management method, people begin to protect themselves.

Team members delay bad news, soften risks, or spend their time proving that they are not at fault. The manager may appear to receive more information, while the information actually becomes slower, less complete, and less useful for decision-making.

Reversal leadership begins by reframing the problem. Instead of asking only who is responsible, ask:

- Which process is creating the obstacle?
- Which information is not moving quickly enough?
- What resources or decision rights are missing?
- Which goals are in conflict?
- What obstacle can the leader remove?

This is not an escape from accountability. It is a way to place accountability where it can improve the result.

## 3. Restore Trust Before Demanding Speed

A team that has been under pressure for a long time cannot be repaired simply by asking it to move faster. Speed must be built on trust. Otherwise, “faster” may only mean producing mistakes more quickly.

A leader trying to reverse a difficult situation can begin with three questions in one-to-one conversations:

1. **What is the biggest obstacle right now?**
2. **Which problem do you hesitate to raise in a meeting?**
3. **What can I help remove?**

The value of these questions is not that they are novel. Their value is the message they send: the leader is not present only to inspect progress. The leader is also willing to understand the frontline reality and accept responsibility for removing obstacles.

When people know that raising a risk will not automatically lead to punishment, information appears earlier. The earlier a problem becomes visible, the lower the cost of correction is likely to be. The team also avoids waiting until a small issue becomes a crisis.

## 4. Transparency Is Not Weakness

Another core element of reversal leadership is transparency with customers, colleagues, and other stakeholders.

Transparency does not mean publishing every unfinished task or creating anxiety without a plan. Useful transparency has three parts:

- **Current state:** What is actually happening?
- **Impact:** Which goals or stakeholders are affected?
- **Action:** What will happen next, and when will the next update arrive?

Instead of saying, “Everything is still under control,” a leader might say:

> “The schedule is behind because the requirements are not yet stable. We will pause new requests, confirm the priorities, and provide a revised plan within two working days.”

This statement may not create instant comfort, but it enables better decisions. Trust does not come from reporting good news all the time. It comes from keeping communication and subsequent action consistent.

## 5. The Real Value of Cross-Industry Experience

Peluso's career also illustrates that the value of cross-industry experience is not simply holding more job titles. It is learning to recognize common problems beneath different industry languages.

Travel businesses may emphasize real-time service and customer experience. E-commerce companies may emphasize speed, experimentation, and conversion. Financial services organizations may emphasize risk, scale, and governance. The language changes, but many management challenges remain similar:

- How can information reach decision-makers faster?
- How can an organization maintain customer trust as it grows?
- How much judgment should frontline employees have?
- How can short-term performance be balanced with long-term capability?

A strong leader does not carry only the answers from the previous job. A strong leader carries better questions. That makes it possible to observe a new environment, test assumptions, and design action rather than imposing a familiar template.

## 6. Four Actions for Today

If you are leading a team under pressure, try these four actions today.

### 1. Turn a blame question into an obstacle question

Do not begin with, “Why is this not finished?” Begin with, “What is the biggest obstacle right now?”

### 2. Create an early channel for bad news

Make it explicit that raising a risk early is responsible behavior, not proof of failure.

### 3. Put the next step in every update

Even when there is no good news, explain the current state, the impact, and the next checkpoint.

### 4. Protect the real priorities

A reversal cannot solve every problem at once. Leaders must help the team identify the few priorities that matter most and pause low-value work.

## Conclusion: Turn Pressure into Trust

Reversal leadership is not a heroic story built around one dramatic decision. It is usually made of ordinary actions that require consistency: listening to the frontline, acknowledging reality, explaining impact, removing obstacles, providing updates, and keeping promises under pressure.

> **Leadership is not about having all the answers. It is about creating enough safety for the team to find the answers together.**

A leader does not need to pretend to know everything. But the leader does need to create an environment where problems can be seen, different opinions can be raised, and people are willing to share responsibility for the outcome.

When a team feels safe enough to tell the truth again, the reversal has truly begun.
